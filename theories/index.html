THEORIES & EVIDENCE

MILGRAM does not always give us answers. Sometimes, it gives us just enough information to make us ask better questions. Throughout the Trials, countless details have been presented to us—some obvious, some hidden, and some that only become meaningful after looking at them again. A lyric may contradict a visual. A character's actions may tell a different story from their words. Something that seemed insignificant during the first Trial may suddenly become important later. And this is where theories begin. This section gathers the different interpretations and ideas that can be formed from the evidence presented throughout MILGRAM. Some theories may explain certain details perfectly, while others may fall apart when new information is considered. Not every theory has to be correct. Sometimes, the most interesting question is simply: “What if we're wrong?”

THEORY 01

MILGRAM IS NOT A PRISON, BUT A PURGATORY

What if MILGRAM isn't actually a prison? This is probably one of the biggest theories surrounding MILGRAM, and honestly, I think it makes a lot of sense when you start looking at the repeated imagery throughout the project. The basic idea is that the prisoners are already dead, and MILGRAM is some sort of purgatory where they are being judged for their sins before they can move on. At first, I didn't really think much of this theory. MILGRAM already presents itself as a prison, and the whole point of the project is that we're supposed to judge ten people who committed crimes. It isn't particularly strange for a fictional prison to have mysterious rules and strange circumstances surrounding it. However, there are way too many references to death, judgment, and the possibility of an afterlife for me to completely dismiss the idea.

There are several instances throughout MILGRAM where the prisoners are associated with imagery that can be interpreted as their deaths. This becomes especially interesting when looking at Undercover, where each prisoner is shown alongside imagery that seems to represent either their crime or their victim. Some of these images can also be interpreted as the circumstances surrounding their own deaths. Yuno, for example, is shown around imagery involving heights and falling. Haruka has imagery associated with water and drowning. Other prisoners have similarly suggestive imagery connected to their own circumstances. Individually, I don't think any of these are enough to prove that they died. But MILGRAM is a project where repetition matters. If one character has death imagery, it could just be symbolism for their crime. If several characters have imagery that can be interpreted as their own deaths, though, I think it becomes much harder to ignore. This leads me to my next claim.
One of the strange things about the prisoners is how little information they initially seem to have about their circumstances. They're aware that they're in MILGRAM, but the actual reason behind their imprisonment is something that has to be uncovered through their memories, their songs, their voice dramas, and the information given to us. If MILGRAM were simply taking living criminals and placing them in a strange prison, this could just be part of the mystery surrounding the facility. But if MILGRAM is some sort of purgatory, then the memory problem becomes much more interesting. They aren't simply prisoners who have forgotten something. They could be dead people who haven't completely accepted that they're dead. And this would explain why so much of MILGRAM feels disconnected from normal reality. The prison itself doesn't behave like an ordinary prison. The prisoners are given music videos that seem to represent their own perspectives. Their crimes aren't explained to us directly. Instead, we're given fragments and symbolism and expected to piece everything together ourselves. This doesn't feel like a normal criminal investigation. It feels more like judgment. And that's important because the entire purpose of purgatory, at least in the religious/philosophical concept, is connected to judgment and purification after death. There are different interpretations of what purgatory actually means, but the general idea involves an intermediate state connected to a person's moral condition before their final destination. MILGRAM seems to take that idea and turn it into a literal voting system. We are the judges. The prisoners are the ones being judged. Their crimes are the sins. And the prison becomes the place where that judgment takes place. But then there's the question of what happens after the judgment. If MILGRAM is purgatory, then the Innocent and Guilty verdicts would have a much bigger meaning than simply determining whether a prisoner gets punished inside the prison. An Innocent verdict could represent forgiveness. A Guilty verdict could represent condemnation. And if the prisoners are already dead, then what we're deciding may not be whether they deserve to remain in MILGRAM. We could be deciding what happens to them after MILGRAM. This would also make the idea of forgiveness much more important. We're not simply being asked, “Did this person commit a crime?” We already know that they did. We're being asked whether they deserve forgiveness despite what they have done. That's basically the entire foundation of the voting system. And I think this is where the purgatory theory becomes particularly interesting.
Those are two completely different questions. Someone can commit a crime and still be forgiven. Someone can have understandable circumstances and still be held accountable. Someone can hurt another person without intending to, and yet still have to live with the consequences. That's exactly the kind of moral judgment that makes sense in a story about purgatory. However, there is one problem with this theory. Not everything fits perfectly. There have been pieces of information that suggest some prisoners may have continued living after the events surrounding their crimes. Yuno is especially interesting because her information has been interpreted as showing that she survived her fall and returned to normal life for some period of time before being brought into MILGRAM. This has caused a lot of discussion over whether the purgatory theory can actually explain every prisoner. But I don't think that completely kills the theory. If MILGRAM is purgatory, it doesn't necessarily mean that every prisoner has to have died in the exact same way or at the exact same point in time. There are already different interpretations of the theory, including ideas where only some of the prisoners are dead. So, I don't think the theory should be treated as “all ten prisoners definitely died and MILGRAM is heaven or hell.” That's too simple. I think the more interesting interpretation is that MILGRAM exists somewhere between life and death, and that the prisoners' relationship with death may be different for each of them. This would also explain why the project is so obsessed with the idea of memories. We are constantly being shown the prisoners' pasts, but rarely in a completely straightforward way. We have to look through their perspective, their songs, and their symbolism to understand what happened. If MILGRAM is some kind of afterlife, then maybe the prisoners aren't simply remembering their crimes. Maybe they're remembering the lives they had before they arrived. And maybe the reason these memories are so distorted is because they are no longer alive to experience them normally. There is another thing that makes this theory interesting to me.
Why are we the ones judging them? Es is physically inside the prison, but the actual judgment is determined by people outside of it. We don't personally know these prisoners. We weren't there when their crimes happened. We only have what MILGRAM chooses to show us. And yet we're still expected to decide whether they deserve forgiveness. That's almost exactly what a judgment after death would look like from the perspective of the story. We don't have perfect information. We don't know everything they did. We don't know every circumstance surrounding their crimes. And yet we're still given the responsibility of deciding. So, are the prisoners actually dead? I don't think there is enough evidence for me to confidently say that all ten of them are. But I also don't think the death imagery is something we should simply ignore. There are too many repeated references to death, judgment, victims, and possible afterlife symbolism for me to completely dismiss the possibility. If MILGRAM really is purgatory, then the prisoners aren't simply waiting for their punishment. They're waiting for us. Waiting to see whether we will forgive them. Waiting to see whether they deserve another chance. And perhaps, when the final judgment is made, we aren't deciding who gets out of prison. We're deciding who gets to move on.
THEORY 02

ES IS THE 11TH PRISONER

Es has no memory of anything besides Milgram. This is pretty much a given, because they’ve said that they have amnesia. However, I think that this is perhaps more intense than at a first glance. Obviously, Es lacks knowledge of their own biographical information. They say that they think they’re 15, and in their character introduction, they repeat that their name is Es and that they’re a prison guard as though looking for an answer of some kind. In Mu’s Audio Drama, they also indicate that they’re not sure about their surname, or if they’re even Japanese. However, I think that Es’s amnesia goes past simply forgetting biographical information. In Shidou’s Audio Drama, he implies that Es doesn’t know about the real world outside of Milgram. However, this seems odd, because Es is in charge of the supply list and gets things the prisoners request. To me, it almost feels like this is saying that Es has never known the real world, even in their forgotten memories. In Amane’s Audio Drama, there’s a moment where Es even seems to doubt if they’re human. Es wakes up in Milgram in the video, “Jackalope’s ‘This is the Milgram.’” Jackalope says all they need to know is that they’re a prison guard, and that they’re here to judge the prisoners. Es takes to this very well. However, Jackalope says some suspicious things too.

In Es’s Audio Drama, when Es says that they don’t really have memories, Jackalope says “that’s always the case.” Obviously, this implies that Jackalope has worked with other prison guards, and in that case, Milgram has likely happened multiple times in the past. I believe that this may be touched on in the novel, but unfortunately, I can’t read it, so I can’t say much more on that. However, related to this is the existence of an eleventh prison cell. In the panopticon, Es notices that there’s an extra room. You can see it in the layout Jackalope presents in “This is Milgram". There’s very clearly a prison cell labeled “000.” In Es’s Audio Drama, they note that it looks very rusty, and that unlike the other cells, there’s no lock on the outside. So, what is this prison cell used for? Es is Prisoner 000. It makes a lot of sense. In the order, which goes in order of prisoner numbers, we get Es first: their MV, their introduction, everything. Their intro is a bit different than everyone else’s, because it doesn’t have the glitched out line at the end–but their image does still get glitchy for a fraction of a second at 0:38. Plus, if the 000 cell is intended for wardens, it makes sense that it wouldn’t have an outside lock, because the warden would be able to let themself out as they please. Furthemore, there’s a key part of Undercover that makes it really seem like Es is prisoner 000. After the Milgram text pops up, it shows them holding a box, which they then open. After they open this box, which is labeled 000, in the same way that the prison cell is, it launches into the whole series of visuals of prisoners and of Milgram in general. At the very end of the video, Es closes the box while Jackalope watches. I think that this is meant to indicate to us that Es is Prisoner 000; their prison is Milgram as a whole, rather than a specific cell. But, if Es is a prisoner, then what is their crime? Before I answer that question, I want to address something else important.
This is what I gather from Es’s MV. It’s not completely clear where, in universe, Es’s MV comes from. For the other prisoners, it’s clear that the MV extraction machine was used in order to get a song, but did this happen to Es as well? In any case, the only thing that we see within Es’s MV is them going over the prisoners. The lyrics of the chorus ask the question of if we can really judge the prisoners for their sins. That’s the crime Es will commit: judging people for their sins. That leads me into what I think will happen: Those voted guilty in the end will die. It’s the only thing that really makes sense to me. We’re dealing with murders directly, and so death seems like the obvious punishment. In Shidou’s Audio Drama, he even says that Milgram smells like death, implying that death occurs here, and given his track record, I’m inclined to believe him. Additionally, it makes Es’s involvement as Prisoner 000 in Milgram make sense. We can’t vote on them yet, because they’re not yet involved in a murder. However, assuming we vote at least one prisoner guilty in the end, Es will be responsible for a death, just like all the other prisoners. Futa hints at this in his audio drama, too. He says that Es imprisoning them is a crime, too. I believe that this is meant to be a hint to the audience, that Es is, too, responsible for their actions. After the Third Trial, I believe we will vote on Es. This is largely based on conjecture and a path of logic from my earlier points, but I think that, at the end of the Third Trial, we’ll see the results of our votes. Those who were voted guilty will die, and if I had to guess, those who were voted innocent will be launched back into society as they are, for better or worse. Then, after seeing the results of our actions, Jackalope will be there to ask us, the audience: did we do a good job? Did we have the right to assign these judgments to these people? I think that that’s the real question Milgram is getting at, because it’s very tied into the Milgram Experiments in real life. The real-life Milgram Experiments sought to see how far people would be willing to inflict pain if someone told them to; with our votes, we are telling Es how to inflict their judgment, and they comply without question. /div>
THEORY 03

MUU'S MURDER IS AN ACCIDENT (MU 3RD ANNIVERSAY OFFICIAL ART)

I have to admit, I originally thought Muu's crime was probably an accident because of the shoe. And honestly, I think that's exactly why this detail is so interesting. At first glance, the missing shoe seems like a very simple piece of evidence. Muu is shown after the confrontation with Rei, one of her shoes is missing, and there are already several theories suggesting that she may have tripped while holding the box cutter. If that's what happened, then the entire situation changes. Muu didn't necessarily decide to murder Rei. She could have been in a desperate confrontation, lost her balance, and accidentally stabbed her. But the more I looked into the shoe, the less I think the important part is simply “Muu tripped.” I think the shoe may be telling us something else.

This theory has been around for a while, and the missing shoe is one of the biggest pieces of evidence people use for it. In It's Not My Fault, Muu is shown with one shoe missing after the confrontation. Fans have pointed out that removing shoes is associated with suicide imagery in Japan, and MILGRAM itself has used missing-shoe imagery in discussions of death and suicide. And suddenly, the shoe becomes much more interesting. Because why would MILGRAM specifically show us that? It would have been extremely easy to simply show Muu standing there after the murder. Instead, we get the missing shoe. And this isn't the only thing that makes me think suicide is important to Muu's story. The lyrics and overall imagery of After Pain are filled with desperation. Muu repeatedly expresses that she doesn't want the situation to continue and that she wants things to disappear. There is a very strong sense that she has reached a point where she doesn't know how to fix what has happened to her. Then we get the line: “Counterattack being a suicide note.” That line is extremely important to me. Because if her final act was simply to murder Rei out of revenge, why describe that act using the language of a suicide note? A suicide note is something left behind by someone who intends to die. So what if Muu's original intention wasn't to kill Rei at all? What if she intended to kill herself? This is where I think the missing shoe connects to the rest of Muu's story. Muu had already lost everything that gave her a sense of control. Her friends turned against her. Her social position collapsed. The person she believed she could control refused to give her what she wanted. And then she is left completely alone. For someone whose entire identity seems to revolve around control, I think that would be devastating. If Muu couldn't control her friends anymore, couldn't control Rei, and couldn't control how everyone viewed her, there was only one thing she could still control: herself. Her own death. And that's where I think the box cutter becomes important. A lot of people interpret the box cutter as proof that Muu went after Rei intending to kill her. And I understand why. She is holding a weapon, she follows Rei, and Rei ends up dead. But that doesn't necessarily tell us what Muu originally intended to do with it. What if she brought it because she intended to hurt herself? What if she wanted Rei to see it? What if “counterattack being a suicide note” means that Muu intended her own death to be the final message she left for Rei? It would be an extremely unhealthy and manipulative thing to do, but I think that actually fits Muu's character better than simply saying she calmly planned a murder. She doesn't know how to communicate with Rei anymore. She doesn't know how to get her friends back. She doesn't know how to make the situation normal again. So she chooses something extreme. She threatens to disappear. And then something goes wrong. This is where the “accident” interpretation comes in. I don't think the missing shoe necessarily proves that Muu literally tripped and accidentally stabbed Rei. In fact, I think that interpretation might be too literal. The shoe could be symbolic of the fact that Muu herself was supposed to be the victim. She may have been prepared to die, and the confrontation with Rei somehow turned that intention into a murder instead. There are several different versions of this theory. Some people believe Muu literally lost her balance and stabbed Rei by accident. Others think she intended to kill herself and then attacked Rei impulsively when Rei rejected her. Others think the shoe simply represents suicidal intent rather than describing the exact physical movements of the murder. Personally, I think the third interpretation is the most interesting.
It tells us something about Muu's state of mind. And this is important because Muu's story becomes much more complicated if she was suicidal. She isn't simply a bully who murdered someone because she was angry. She's someone who was already in an extremely unstable state, who had just lost the social world she depended on, and who may have been considering her own death. Then Rei becomes involved. And somehow, instead of Muu dying, Rei does. That's horrifying. Because now Muu has to live with exactly what she may have been trying to escape from. She wanted everything to disappear. Instead, someone else disappeared. And I think this may explain why Muu's behavior in It's Not My Fault is so strange. She constantly insists that she isn't at fault. She wants Es to tell her that she's innocent. She wants someone else to confirm that she isn't responsible for what happened. If she genuinely believes that she never intended for Rei to die, then admitting responsibility would mean confronting the fact that her actions caused the death of someone she may have cared about. So she denies it. “It's not my fault.” Maybe this isn't simply Muu being arrogant. Maybe it's how she protects herself from the truth. There's also the fact that Muu and Rei's relationship seems much more complicated than “bully and victim.” The later material gives us more context for how Muu's behavior affected Rei and how their relationship developed, which makes the final confrontation much harder to interpret as a simple planned murder. Muu clearly did terrible things to Rei. I don't think the suicide theory should erase that. This is actually where I disagree with the idea that “if Muu's murder was accidental, then she must be innocent.” Those aren't automatically the same thing. Someone can cause a death without intending to kill. Someone can behave horribly toward another person and still not have planned their death. Someone can be responsible for creating the circumstances that eventually lead to a tragedy without having intended the final result. That's what makes Muu so difficult to judge. And honestly, I think that's the point. If the shoe really is connected to suicide imagery, then maybe MILGRAM isn't telling us: “Muu accidentally tripped.” Maybe it's telling us: “Muu was already falling apart before Rei died.” And I think that's much more interesting. Because then the missing shoe isn't just evidence of a physical accident. It's evidence of a mental state. A person who wanted to disappear. A person who wanted control over something. A person who may have intended for herself to become the victim. And somehow, when the confrontation was over, she was still alive. Rei wasn't. So, did Muu intend to kill Rei? I don't think the shoe alone can answer that. Did she intend to die? I think there is enough imagery for me to seriously consider it. Was Rei's death an accident? Maybe. But I don't think the important question is whether Muu literally tripped. I think the important question is what Muu intended before everything went wrong. Because if her intention was to die, then the story isn't simply about a murderer killing her victim. It's about someone who was already destroying herself, only to destroy someone else instead. And that makes Muu's crime much more tragic than the shoe theory initially made me think.
