VERDICTS

We have listened to their stories. We have examined their crimes. We have spent three Trials trying to understand them. Now comes the part that MILGRAM has asked of us from the very beginning: Judge them. Every vote carries a consequence. A prisoner may be forgiven because we believe their actions can be understood, or condemned because we believe their crime cannot be excused. But judgment is rarely that simple. As new information appears, our opinions may change. A prisoner we once considered unforgivable may begin to look different. Someone we initially sympathized with may leave us questioning our decision. This section records the judgments throughout the Trials and the reasoning behind them. There is no judge waiting to correct us. The verdict is ours.

MY VERDICT

MILGRAM asks us whether its prisoners can be forgiven, but after three trials, I do not think a simple INNOCENT or GUILTY vote can fully describe every case. Knowing what happened is one thing; deciding how much responsibility a person should carry for it is another.

These are my own final judgments based on how I understood each prisoner's crime, circumstances, intentions, and the people involved. They are not meant to replace MILGRAM's official verdicts. Rather, this is where I stop looking at how everyone else voted and answer the question for myself: if the judgment were entirely mine, would I forgive them?

001

HARUKA SAKURAI

FINAL VERDICT
INNOCENT
MY REASONING

I know Haruka is the person who physically committed the murders, but I still cannot bring myself to place the majority of the blame on him. His actions did not develop in isolation. Haruka spent most of his life desperately seeking attention, affection, and acknowledgement from a mother who continuously made him feel as though there was something inherently wrong with him.

That does not magically make killing acceptable. However, when I judge Haruka, I cannot ignore the environment that shaped him into the person who eventually committed those acts. He was a neglected child who learned that hurting something could finally make people look at him. Had he been given the care, attention, guidance, and intervention he clearly needed much earlier, I genuinely do not think his story would have ended the same way.

So yes, the hands that committed the crime were Haruka's, but I think much of the responsibility belongs to the adults who failed him long before MILGRAM ever judged him. His mother may not have committed the murder herself, but her neglect is impossible for me to separate from what Haruka eventually became. For that reason, my verdict is INNOCENT—not because nothing happened, but because I do not believe Haruka alone deserves to carry the full weight of why it happened.

002

YUNO KASHIKI

FINAL VERDICT
INNOCENT
MY REASONING

Yuno is probably one of the easiest INNOCENT votes for me because I do not personally consider the loss of her pregnancy enough reason to label her a murderer. Even if abortion had been involved, I still would not automatically equate that decision with murder. More importantly, my interpretation of Yuno's story is not even that simple.

From the way I understand her circumstances, the pregnancy itself appears to have been unintended. Protection can fail regardless of how careful someone is, and an unwanted pregnancy does not suddenly make the pregnant person morally responsible for everything that follows. What matters more to me is how badly the entire situation affected Yuno herself.

I interpret her story as someone reaching such an emotionally dangerous point that she herself could have died, only for the pregnancy to be the life that was ultimately lost instead. That is tragic, but tragedy and murder are not automatically the same thing. I cannot look at Yuno's situation and see a malicious killer who deliberately decided that another person needed to die. I see a young woman whose circumstances spiraled into something devastating. That is nowhere near enough for me to condemn her. INNOCENT.

003

FUUTA KAJIYAMA

FINAL VERDICT
GUILTY
MY REASONING

Fuuta is where my sympathy starts running out. I understand that he did not necessarily begin with the intention of killing anyone. He convinced himself that what he was doing online was justice, almost treating real people like enemies in a game rather than individuals who could actually be hurt. The problem is that there were multiple opportunities for him to stop.

His target apologized. The situation could have ended there. Instead, Fuuta continued participating in the harassment because he had already decided that she was the villain and that punishing her was justified. And what was the original offense supposed to deserve that level of treatment? From my perspective, practically nothing. Someone made a mistake that Fuuta and his group considered worthy of public punishment, and they escalated it until a real person could no longer endure the consequences.

What frustrates me even more is that Fuuta clearly becomes horrified once the consequences finally become real to him. I believe that guilt is genuine—but remorse after someone's death does not erase the choices that helped cause it. He wanted accountability when he was the one delivering it to other people; therefore, I think he has to accept accountability when the same standard is turned toward him. GUILTY.

004

MUU KUSONOKI

FINAL VERDICT
GUILTY
MY REASONING

Muu is complicated because she absolutely was bullied, and I am not going to pretend that what happened to her was harmless. The problem is that being victimized does not erase the fact that she had previously participated in the exact same kind of cruelty toward someone else.

That is what makes her situation so frustrating to me. When Muu had power and social protection, Rei was the one suffering. Muu did not suddenly recognize how horrible bullying was when Rei needed help. It only became completely unbearable once the social hierarchy changed and Muu became the person at the bottom of it. I can sympathize with how painful that must have been while still recognizing the hypocrisy involved.

Rei refusing to rescue Muu from the consequences of that social collapse also does not make Rei responsible for Muu's suffering. Muu expected understanding from someone she had previously hurt, and when she did not receive it, Rei ultimately became the person who died. Whether the stabbing involved panic, impulse, or circumstances more complicated than straightforward premeditation, Rei still lost her life in a conflict Muu helped create.

Muu can be both a victim and someone who seriously harmed another person. Those identities do not cancel each other out. I feel sorry for what happened to her, but sympathy is not the same thing as forgiveness. GUILTY.

005

SHIDOU KIRISAKI

FINAL VERDICT
UNDECIDED
MY REASONING

Shidou is one of the prisoners I genuinely cannot reduce to INNOCENT or GUILTY without feeling like I am ignoring half of his story. If his actions involved sacrificing or exploiting other patients in an attempt to save his own family, then yes, that is morally horrifying. Being desperate does not mean another person's life suddenly becomes less valuable.

At the same time, I understand exactly where that desperation came from. Shidou was a doctor who had spent his career trying to keep people alive, and then he was placed in a situation where his own family became the people he desperately wanted to save. I cannot imagine how devastating it would be to possess the knowledge and skills to save lives every day and still be unable to save the people you love most.

And after everything, he is left with almost nothing. He loses his family, has to confront what he did while trying to preserve them, and is still expected to continue living with that knowledge. His actions may have crossed a serious ethical line, but I cannot look at him and see someone motivated by cruelty, selfish pleasure, or indifference toward human life.

So this is one of the few cases where I genuinely refuse to force myself into either side. Legally and ethically, I understand a GUILTY judgment. Emotionally, I understand why people forgive him. Apparently I have been sentenced to feeling sorry for Shidou forever. UNDECIDED.

006

MAHIRU SHIINA

FINAL VERDICT
INNOCENT
MY REASONING

I cannot call Mahiru a murderer simply because the person she loved eventually took his own life. Was Mahiru's way of loving overwhelming? Absolutely. She could be dependent, obsessive, emotionally intense, and completely unaware of how suffocating that intensity could become for another person. But being a difficult or unhealthy partner is not automatically equivalent to killing someone.

A relationship requires communication from both people. If Mahiru's partner could no longer handle the relationship, then there were other possible responses: confronting her, establishing boundaries, asking for distance, or ending the relationship completely. His suffering deserves to be taken seriously, but his suicide cannot simply be transferred onto Mahiru as though she personally made the final decision for him.

What makes the situation even sadder is that Mahiru genuinely seems to interpret everything through love. Her problem was not that she wanted him dead; it was that she loved in a way that had become unhealthy for both of them and apparently did not understand the damage until it was already irreversible.

She may need to understand boundaries. She may need to understand that love cannot survive through devotion alone. But murderer? No. I cannot give her that label. INNOCENT. #JusticeForMahiru because apparently wanting to love somebody has somehow gotten this woman imprisoned.

007

KAZUI MUKUHARA

FINAL VERDICT
INNOCENT
MY REASONING

Kazui's story hurts because I do not think there was ever going to be a version of this situation where nobody felt betrayed. His wife had every right to be devastated. Imagine building years of marriage around the belief that someone loved you romantically, only to discover that an enormous part of the relationship was built around something your partner could never fully give you. I completely understand why that revelation destroyed her.

But I also understand Kazui. Living openly as yourself is already difficult now; depending on his environment, generation, family expectations, and social pressures, admitting the truth about his sexuality could have meant risking practically everything. That does not make the deception harmless, but it does explain why someone could spend years desperately trying to perform the life expected of him.

His wife's death is therefore tragic without requiring Kazui to be her murderer. Telling the truth hurt her, but continuing the lie would also have meant forcing both of them to spend the rest of their lives inside a relationship that was fundamentally dishonest. There was no fair winner here.

I do think Kazui bears responsibility for the years of deception and for the emotional consequences it created. However, I do not believe he deserves to carry responsibility for another person's suicide simply because his truth became part of what preceded it. They both deserved a life where neither of them had to live inside that lie. INNOCENT.

008

AMANE MOMOSE

FINAL VERDICT
INNOCENT
MY REASONING

Amane is a child. That fact alone changes the entire way I judge her case. She was not simply taught a few strange religious beliefs and then independently decided to become violent. She was raised inside an environment that controlled her understanding of morality from the beginning and punished her whenever she demonstrated compassion outside of what that belief system permitted.

What makes her case especially disturbing is that the adults around her were supposed to be the people teaching her right from wrong. Instead, they abused her until obedience became synonymous with goodness. When a child is repeatedly punished for questioning authority and rewarded for following harmful doctrine, eventually that child is going to reproduce the morality she was taught.

This does not mean the death connected to Amane suddenly stops mattering. Someone still died. But when I ask who created the conditions that made that death possible, my attention immediately goes to the adults who indoctrinated and abused her. They created the rules. They taught her punishment. They taught her what supposedly deserved condemnation.

Then, when those same beliefs eventually turned against them, Amane became the murderer while the system responsible for shaping her disappeared conveniently into the background. I refuse to judge a child by the exact moral framework abusive adults forced into her. INNOCENT.

009

MIKOTO KAYANO

FINAL VERDICT
UNDECIDED
MY REASONING

Mikoto is probably the hardest prisoner for me to judge because responsibility becomes extremely complicated once John is involved. Multiple innocent people appear to have died, and that cannot simply be ignored. Regardless of which alter committed the violence, those victims are still dead.

However, I also cannot comfortably punish Mikoto for actions he may genuinely have had no awareness or control over. Having Dissociative Identity Disorder would not automatically make a person innocent, nor does a mental health condition erase responsibility by itself. The important question for me is specifically what Mikoto knew, what John did, when Mikoto became aware of it, and how much agency either of them actually possessed at each point.

And that is exactly why I cannot vote confidently. If I judge only the result, multiple deaths make GUILTY seem obvious. If I judge Mikoto's personal intention and awareness, however, condemning him for murders committed during periods he apparently could not even remember feels deeply uncomfortable.

More than punishment, this entire situation screams intervention. Mikoto needs professional psychiatric support, stability, and treatment—not a supernatural prison repeatedly screaming GUILTY or INNOCENT at him and somehow expecting that to solve anything. Until I can separate responsibility from circumstance more confidently, my verdict stays UNDECIDED.

010

KOTOKO YUZURIHA

FINAL VERDICT
UNDECIDED
MY REASONING

Kotoko is difficult because I understand the anger that created her. There are people who hurt others and escape consequences completely. There are victims who never receive the justice they deserve. When institutions repeatedly fail vulnerable people, I understand why someone like Kotoko could eventually decide, "Fine. I'll do it myself."

And part of me sympathizes with that. If she targets people who genuinely harmed others, especially people who exploited those weaker than them, it is difficult not to understand why victims or observers might see her as someone finally delivering the justice nobody else would.

But this is also exactly what scares me about her. Kotoko appoints herself investigator, judge, jury, and executioner. Once one person decides that their own morality gives them the authority to determine who deserves violence, there is no independent system left to stop them when they are wrong. And MILGRAM itself demonstrates the danger of that mentality: judging people becomes much easier than understanding them.

I can understand why Kotoko acts. I can even sympathize with what she wants to accomplish. What I cannot comfortably accept is one individual possessing the absolute authority to decide which human beings deserve punishment or death. Justice matters, but justice without limits can become another form of violence. That is why, even after three trials, I still cannot completely forgive or completely condemn her. UNDECIDED.
